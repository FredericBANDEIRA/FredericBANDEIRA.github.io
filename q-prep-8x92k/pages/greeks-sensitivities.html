Δ Delta

Q-GS-01
Answered
What is the delta of an option? Give the mathematical definition.
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Definition

The delta (Δ) of an option represents the sensitivity of the option's price to a $1 change in the price of the underlying asset. It tells you how much the premium will move if the spot price moves by one unit.

📌 Mathematical Definition

Mathematically, it is the first partial derivative of the option's price (V) with respect to the underlying asset's spot price (S):

Δ = ∂V / ∂S

Q-GS-02
Answered
What is the sign of your delta when you are: long call, long put, short call, short put?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Delta Signs

  • Long Call: Positive (+) — As the stock goes up, your call gains value.
  • Long Put: Negative (-) — As the stock goes up, your put loses value.
  • Short Call: Negative (-) — You are short the call, so if the stock goes up, the liability increases (you lose money).
  • Short Put: Positive (+) — You are short the put, so if the stock goes up, the put loses value and your liability decreases (you make money).
Q-GS-03
Answered
What is the delta of a call ATM? Between what values can the delta of a call fluctuate? And for a put?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 ATM Delta

The delta of an At-The-Money (ATM) Call is slightly above 0.5 (e.g., ~0.52 to 0.55). It is not exactly 0.5 due to the log-normal distribution of stock prices in the Black-Scholes model, which slightly skews the probability. The delta of an ATM Put is slightly above -0.5 (closer to zero, e.g., ~-0.48).

📌 Fluctuations (Bounds)

  • Call Option Delta: Fluctuates between 0 (deep OTM) and 1 (deep ITM).
  • Put Option Delta: Fluctuates between -1 (deep ITM) and 0 (deep OTM).
Q-GS-04
Answered
Draw the graph of delta as a function of the spot price. How does the delta evolve from deep OTM to deep ITM?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Evolution of Delta

For a Call option, the delta evolves in an "S-curve" (sigmoid) shape as the underlying spot price increases. It starts near 0 (when deep OTM), accelerates as it approaches the strike (crossing ~0.5 at ATM), and flattens out near 1 (when deep ITM).

📈 Call Delta vs Spot Price

📌 Note on Maturity

As the option gets closer and closer to maturity, this S-curve becomes steeper and steeper, eventually converging into a strict step-function exactly at maturity (Delta is exactly 0 if OTM, and exactly 1 if ITM).

Q-GS-05
Answered
Can the delta of an option ever exceed the notional of the option?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Vanilla Options vs Exotics

For a standard vanilla option (Call or Put), the answer is No. The absolute value of delta is strictly bounded between 0 and 1. The option cannot move more than 1-for-1 with the underlying asset.

However, for exotic options (like Digital / Binary options or Barrier options), the answer is Yes! For a digital option right at the strike price moments before expiration, the payoff jumps discontinuously from 0 to 1. This means an infinitesimally small change in the spot price causes a massive change in the option's value. The delta approaches infinity (Δ > 1) at that specific point.

Q-GS-06
Answered
What is delta hedging? Why must you continuously readjust your delta hedge?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Delta Hedging

Delta hedging is the process of neutralizing the directional risk of an options position by taking an offsetting position in the underlying asset. The goal is to make the total portfolio delta zero (Δ = 0), ensuring the portfolio's value doesn't change for small moves in the underlying spot price.

📌 Why Continuous Readjustment? (Dynamic Hedging)

Because delta is not constant! As the underlying spot price moves, the delta of the option changes (this rate of change is called Gamma). Therefore, a hedge ratio that is perfectly delta-neutral today will no longer be neutral tomorrow if the stock price has moved. To remain perfectly delta-neutral, a trader must constantly buy or sell the underlying asset to readjust the hedge.

Q-GS-07
Answered
I short 100 calls at 50% delta on Apple. How do I hedge?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Calculation

Your position is short calls, so your delta is negative. Specifically:
-100 (calls) × 0.50 (delta) = -50 total delta.

📌 The Hedge

To hedge a negative 50 delta, you need to add a positive 50 delta to your portfolio. Since 1 share of stock always has exactly 1 delta (it moves 1-for-1 with itself), you simply buy 50 shares of Apple stock.

Q-GS-08
Answered
Exercise: Spot = 125, long call strike 140, delta = 0.4, gamma is flat, premium = 8. The spot moves to 127. What is the new premium?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Calculation

Because gamma is flat (zero), the delta does not change as the underlying moves. The price of the underlying increased by 2 units (from 125 to 127).

  • Change in Option Price = Change in Spot × Delta
  • Change in Option Price = 2 × 0.4 = +0.8

The new premium is the old premium plus the change: 8 + 0.8 = 8.8.

Q-GS-09
Answered
How does the delta of a call ATM change as time to maturity decreases?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The "Anchor" Point

The delta of an At-The-Money (ATM) call is anchored near 0.5. As time to maturity decreases, the ATM delta does not move away from this anchor. It stays roughly at 0.5.

Note: What does change is the slope of the curve around the ATM point. As maturity approaches, the transition from 0 (OTM) to 1 (ITM) becomes much steeper, eventually becoming a vertical step function at expiration. But the midpoint (ATM) stays anchored at ~0.5.

Q-GS-10
Answered
How does the delta of an ITM call change if implied volatility increases?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Decreasing Delta

The delta of an ITM call will decrease (move away from 1 and closer to 0.5) if implied volatility increases.

📌 Why?

Higher volatility means wider price swings. If an option is currently deeply ITM (delta near 1, very high probability of expiring ITM), an increase in volatility means there is now a higher chance that a massive swing could push the underlying spot price all the way down, causing the option to expire OTM. Because the probability of expiring ITM is now less "certain", the delta drops towards 0.5.

(Conversely, for an OTM option, higher volatility increases the chance it might swing into the money, so its delta increases towards 0.5. In short: high vol pulls all deltas towards 0.5).

Γ Gamma

Q-GS-11
Answered
What is gamma? What is its sign when you are long a call? Is it the same for a put?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Definition

Gamma (Γ) measures the rate of change of an option's Delta with respect to a $1 change in the underlying asset's price. Mathematically, it is the second partial derivative of the option's price with respect to the spot price: Γ = ∂²V / ∂S² = ∂Δ / ∂S. It represents the convexity of the option's value relative to the underlying.

📌 Sign of Gamma

Gamma is always positive for any long option position (whether it is a Long Call or a Long Put). Being long an option means you benefit from large moves in the underlying asset, which is what positive gamma represents.

Q-GS-12
Answered
Why is gamma maximum at ATM? (Hint: convexity)
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Maximum at ATM

Gamma represents how fast the delta is changing. Delta changes the most aggressively exactly when the option transitions from being Out-of-the-Money to In-the-Money (the ATM point). Think of the delta S-curve: it is steepest right in the middle.

Economically, ATM options have the highest uncertainty regarding whether they will expire worthless or with intrinsic value. A small move in the spot price drastically shifts that probability, resulting in the highest convexity (gamma).

Q-GS-13
Answered
Draw the graph of gamma. How does it change for a shorter maturity option?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Gamma vs Maturity

Gamma looks like a bell curve centered at the strike price (ATM). As maturity approaches, this bell curve becomes taller and narrower. Exactly at maturity, it collapses into a spike (a Dirac delta function) at the strike, because the delta shifts instantly from 0 to 1.

📉 Gamma vs Spot Price (Maturity Effect)
Q-GS-14
Answered
I am delta neutral and anticipate a significant move in the underlying. How should I position my gamma? What option combinations reproduce such a position?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Long Gamma

If you expect a massive directional move (volatility breakout) but don't know which direction, you want to be Long Gamma while remaining Delta Neutral. Being long gamma means your delta becomes positive as the market rallies (getting you long), and negative as the market crashes (getting you short). You make money dynamically as long as the move is large enough.

📌 Strategies

  • Long Straddle: Buy a Call and Buy a Put at the same ATM strike. (High cost, highest gamma).
  • Long Strangle: Buy an OTM Call and Buy an OTM Put. (Cheaper, but requires an even larger move to become profitable).
Q-GS-15
Answered
Exercise: Delta = 300M€, Gamma = 100M€. The market moves +5%. How does my position evolve?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Evolution of Delta

Gamma is the change in Delta. Assuming Gamma is quoted in cash terms per 1% move:
New Delta = Old Delta + (Gamma × Move)
New Delta = 300M + (100M × 5) = 800M€

📌 Evolution of P&L (Taylor Expansion)

Your total P&L comes from both the linear Delta component and the convex Gamma component (which is squared):
P&L ≈ (Delta × Move) + 0.5 × Gamma × (Move)²
*Assuming the move is expressed as units of 1% (so move = 5).
P&L ≈ (300M × 5) + 0.5 × 100M × (5)²
P&L ≈ 1,500M + 1,250M = +2.75 Billion €

Q-GS-16
Answered
At what frequency do you hedge your delta when you are short gamma? And when you are long gamma?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Short Gamma: High Frequency

When you are short gamma, you lose money on large moves. You are "bleeding" convexity. Therefore, you must hedge as frequently as possible (continuously) to lock in small losses before they compound into massive ones.

📌 Long Gamma: Low Frequency

When you are long gamma, you make money on large moves. You are gaining convexity. You want to let the profits "run" as the market moves away from your strike, so you hedge less frequently (e.g., at the end of the day or only after a large threshold is breached) before resetting your delta to zero.

Q-GS-17
Answered
Why can you never be both gamma neutral and theta neutral at the same time?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Black-Scholes PDE

Gamma and Theta are mathematically tied together via the Black-Scholes PDE. For a delta-neutral portfolio (Δ = 0), the equation simplifies to:

Θ + ½ σ² S² Γ = rV

Assuming interest rates (r) are zero or negligible, Θ ≈ -½ σ² S² Γ. Because variance (σ²) and price squared (S²) are always strictly positive, Theta and Gamma must always have opposite signs.

You either pay Theta (time decay) to hold Gamma (long options), or you collect Theta by taking on Gamma risk (short options). You cannot neutralize both simultaneously without flattening your entire position.

Q-GS-18
Answered
How does gamma change when implied volatility increases?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 It Flattens Out

When implied volatility increases, the gamma bell curve flattens and widens. The peak at ATM decreases, but the "wings" (deep ITM and OTM) slightly increase.

Why? Higher volatility spreads the probability distribution over a much wider range of strikes. Because the stock is now expected to swing wildly, the delta transitions from 0 to 1 much more gradually over a wider price range, meaning the rate of change of delta (Gamma) is lower near the strike but non-zero further away.

Q-GS-19
Answered
What is the gamma of a call spread? How does it differ from a single call?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Two Peaks (One Positive, One Negative)

A Call Spread involves going Long a Call at strike K₁ and Short a Call at strike K₂. Because long calls have positive gamma and short calls have negative gamma, the overall gamma profile has two peaks.

📈 Call Spread Gamma vs Spot

Unlike a single call (which always has positive gamma everywhere), the call spread's gamma flips from positive to negative depending on where the spot price is relative to the two strikes.

ν Vega

Q-GS-20
Answered
What is vega? Is "vega" actually a Greek letter? When you are long a call, is your vega positive or negative?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Definition

Vega measures the sensitivity of an option's price to a 1% change in implied volatility. Mathematically, it is the first derivative of the option price with respect to volatility: ν = ∂V / ∂σ.

📌 Not a Greek Letter!

Vega is not a real Greek letter! The actual Greek letter is Nu (ν). However, traders wanted a term that sounded similar to Delta, Gamma, and Theta, so "Vega" became the industry standard. Sometimes you'll see it called Kappa.

📌 Sign

If you are long an option (Call or Put), your Vega is always positive. Higher volatility increases the probability of the option finishing deep ITM, making it more valuable.

Q-GS-21
Answered
Is vega higher for long or short maturity options? Why?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Higher for Long Maturity

Vega is much larger for longer maturity options.

Why? Because volatility is scaled by the square root of time (σ√T). A 1% increase in volatility matters a lot more when there are 10 years left for the stock to swing wildly, compared to if there is only 1 day left before expiration.

Q-GS-22
Answered
At which strike level is vega the most important?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 At-The-Money (ATM)

Vega is highest exactly At-The-Money (ATM). This is because ATM options consist entirely of extrinsic (time/volatility) value and have the highest uncertainty about whether they will expire ITM or OTM. Therefore, they are the most sensitive to changes in implied volatility.

Q-GS-23
Answered
Exercise: I have a positive vega of 500,000 and volatility changes by +3%. What is my P&L?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Calculation

Vega is traditionally quoted as the P&L change for a 1-point (1%) move in implied volatility.

  • Change in P&L = Vega × Change in Volatility (in points)
  • Change in P&L = 500,000 × 3 = +1,500,000
Q-GS-24
Answered
I am long a call deep ITM. Am I still vega positive?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Vega Approaches Zero

Technically, yes, it is infinitesimally positive. But practically, you are Vega Neutral (Vega ≈ 0).

A deep ITM option acts almost exactly like the underlying stock itself (Delta ≈ 1). It consists almost entirely of intrinsic value, with virtually zero extrinsic (time/volatility) value. Therefore, changes in implied volatility will not affect its price in any meaningful way.

Q-GS-25
Answered
What is the vega of a swap? (Trick question!)
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Zero

A swap is a linear instrument (its payoff profile is a straight line, not a curve). It does not have any optionality or convexity embedded in it. Therefore, it is completely immune to changes in implied volatility. The vega of a swap is exactly 0.

Θ Theta & Rho

Q-GS-26
Answered
What is theta? When you are long a call, is your theta positive or negative? Why does theta increase as you approach maturity?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Definition

Theta (Θ) measures the sensitivity of an option's price to the passage of time. It is the first derivative of the option price with respect to time: Θ = ∂V / ∂t (often called "time decay").

📌 Sign & Acceleration

For a long call (or any long option), theta is negative. You lose money every day you hold the option, assuming nothing else changes. Theta increases in magnitude (the bleeding speeds up) as you approach maturity because the "extrinsic value" of an ATM option drops off a cliff in the final days. The remaining uncertainty collapses rapidly.

Q-GS-27
Answered
I own an ATM straddle. One day passes with no move in the underlying and no change in parameters. Have I made or lost money? Why?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 You Lost Money

An ATM straddle consists of being Long a Call and Long a Put. Both legs of the trade are long options, meaning both legs have negative theta. You paid premium for both, and since a day passed with zero movement, the time value of both options decayed. Your total position lost money (theta bleed).

Q-GS-28
Answered
What is the advantage of being gamma positive? Why prefer it over theta?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Buying Low, Selling High

When you are gamma positive (and delta hedging), the convexity works in your favor. As the stock rallies, your delta becomes positive, meaning you must sell stock to return to delta zero. As the stock crashes, your delta becomes negative, meaning you must buy stock to return to delta zero. You are continuously "buying low and selling high" without even predicting the market direction!

Traders prefer it because it allows them to harvest volatility and make directional-independent profits, provided the actual realized volatility of the stock outpaces the daily theta decay (which is the fixed cost of holding that positive gamma).

📌 Tail Risk Asymmetry (Why prefer Gamma over Theta?)

Traders strongly prefer to be Gamma positive (paying Theta) rather than Theta positive (receiving Theta) because of tail risk. If you are Gamma positive, your downside is strictly capped (you only lose your fixed daily theta bleed), but your upside is theoretically unlimited in the event of a market crash or massive rally. If you are Theta positive (short options), your upside is capped to the premium collected, but your downside is theoretically unlimited—a single "black swan" tail event can wipe out months of collected theta in a single day.

Q-GS-29
Answered
What is rho? Why is a call rho positive and a put rho negative? Explain economically.
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Definition & Economics

Rho (ρ) measures the sensitivity of the option's price to a 1% change in the risk-free interest rate (ρ = ∂V / ∂r).

  • Call (Positive Rho): Buying a call means you have the right to buy the stock later, deferring the payment of the strike price until maturity. If interest rates rise, the cash you kept in the bank (instead of buying the stock today) earns more interest. Therefore, higher rates make calls more valuable.
  • Put (Negative Rho): Buying a put means you have the right to sell the stock and receive cash later. If interest rates rise, you are losing out on the higher interest you could have earned if you received the cash today. Therefore, higher rates make puts less valuable.
Q-GS-30
Answered
I have a call spread. Interest rates increase. What happens to the price of my spread?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Price Increases

A Call Spread consists of being Long K₁ (lower strike) and Short K₂ (higher strike). Rho is generally higher for lower strike/ITM calls because there is a higher probability that the deferred cash (the strike price) will actually exchange hands. Therefore, the positive Rho of the Long K₁ call outweighs the negative Rho of the Short K₂ call. The net position has a positive Rho, meaning an increase in interest rates increases the price of the spread.

📈 Second-Order Greeks & Portfolio Greeks

Q-GS-31
Answered
Name and define the second-order Greeks: Vanna, Volga (Vomma), Charm (Color).
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Second-Order Greeks

  • Vanna (∂Δ / ∂σ or ∂ν / ∂S): Measures how much Delta changes when Implied Volatility changes (or how Vega changes when Spot changes). It captures the skew risk.
  • Volga / Vomma (∂ν / ∂σ or ∂²V / ∂σ²): Measures how much Vega changes when Implied Volatility changes. It represents the convexity of the option price with respect to volatility.
  • Charm / Delta Decay (∂Δ / ∂t): Measures how much Delta changes as time passes (getting closer to expiration).
  • Color (∂Γ / ∂t): Measures how much Gamma changes as time passes (the "charm" of gamma).
Q-GS-32
Answered
You have a portfolio with given delta, gamma, theta, and vega. How do you make it delta neutral? Gamma neutral? Vega neutral?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Neutralization Sequence

You must neutralize from the highest order to the lowest order, because lower-order instruments won't mess up higher-order Greeks:

  1. Vega & Gamma Neutrality: You must use options to neutralize Vega and Gamma (since the underlying asset has 0 Vega and 0 Gamma). Because options have different Vega/Gamma ratios across strikes and maturities, you generally need to trade at least two different options to neutralize both simultaneously.
  2. Delta Neutrality: Once your Vega and Gamma are neutralized using options, your Delta will have changed. You then buy or sell the underlying asset (shares/futures) to bring the new net Delta to zero. Since the underlying has no Vega or Gamma, doing this won't ruin your previous work!
Q-GS-33
Answered
My book is delta neutral. When I increase vol by 1%, I become short delta. What is my position in skew?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Short Vanna (Short Skew)

The sensitivity of Delta to Volatility is called Vanna. If Vol increases and Delta becomes negative (short), then your Vanna is negative (∂Δ / ∂σ < 0).

In equity markets, downside Puts have the highest implied volatility (the "Skew"). A long downside Put has positive Vanna. If your Vanna is negative, it means you are likely short downside Puts. Therefore, your position in the skew is Short Skew.

Q-GS-34
Answered
What is the gamma of a 1-year option with 25% vol and 20K theta?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Black-Scholes PDE

Assuming delta neutrality and zero interest rates, the PDE states: Θ + ½ σ² S² Γ = 0

If we rearrange for the "Cash Gamma" (which is ½ S² Γ):

  • Cash Gamma = -Θ / σ²
  • Assuming the option is long, Theta is negative (-20K).
  • Cash Gamma = -(-20,000) / (0.25)²
  • Cash Gamma = 20,000 / 0.0625 = 320,000

The total cash gamma of the position is roughly 320K.

Q-GS-35
Answered
Exercise: You are long 100K vega and 1M gamma. The market moves +3% and vol increases by 1 point. What is your P&L?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Independent P&L Contributions

Assuming a delta-neutral book, your P&L comes from the Volatility move (Vega) and the Spot move (Gamma):

  • Vega P&L: Vega × dVol = 100K × 1 = +100K
  • Gamma P&L: ½ × Gamma × (dSpot)² = 0.5 × 1M × (3)² = 4.5M
  • Total P&L: 100K + 4.5M = +4.6 Million
