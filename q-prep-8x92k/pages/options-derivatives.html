📌 Call & Put Fundamentals

Q-OD-01
Answered
What is a European call option? What is a European put option? What is the difference with an American option?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Elevator Pitch

A Call option gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy an underlying asset at a pre-specified strike price (K) on or before a specific maturity date (T). In exchange for this right, the buyer pays an upfront cost called the premium (C).

A Put option gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to sell the underlying asset at the strike price (K) on or before maturity (T) for an upfront premium (P).

📐 Payoff at Maturity (T)

Call Payoff = max(0, ST − K)
Put Payoff = max(0, K − ST)

Where ST is the spot price of the underlying at maturity T, and K is the strike price.

🌍 European vs. American Distinction

  • European Option: Can only be exercised precisely at maturity (T).
  • American Option: Can be exercised at any time between today and maturity (t ∈ [0, T]).
💡 Interview Trap & Follow-Up:
Interviewer: "Which option is more expensive, American or European? And when would you actually exercise an American option early?"
Answer: An American option is always worth at least as much as a European option (CAm ≥ CEur) because it gives extra optionality. However, on a non-dividend paying stock, you should never early exercise an American Call (you forfeit the time value and pay cash early, losing interest on K). For an American Put, early exercise can be optimal if deeply in-the-money and interest rates are high (to collect K immediately and earn interest on cash). If the stock pays dividends, an American Call may be exercised right before the ex-dividend date to capture the dividend!

1. Always draw two lines or clearly state the difference between Gross Payoff vs. Net P&L: Gross Payoff starts exactly at 0 on the y-axis and kinks at K. Net P&L shifts the entire graph down by C (or P) for long positions, and up by C (or P) for short positions.
2. Zero-Bound Trap for Puts: Interviewers love to ask: "Is the max profit on a long call and long put both unlimited?"
Answer: No! Long Call has +∞ profit potential because a stock can theoretically rise forever (ST → +∞). But for a Long Put, the stock price cannot fall below $0 (ST ≥ 0), so the maximum gross payoff is bounded at K, making the net maximum profit exactly K − P.
Q-OD-03
Answered
What is the breakeven of an option?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Elevator Pitch & Standard Formula

The breakeven price (SBE) of an option is the underlying spot price at which the net P&L of the position is exactly zero (Net P&L = 0).

  • Call Breakeven (Long or Short): SBE = K + C
  • Put Breakeven (Long or Short): SBE = K − P
🎯 Quant / Trading Desk Distinction (How to score 10/10 in an interview):
While K + C is the textbook formula at expiration, a top-tier candidate must point out two critical nuances:
1. Time Value of Money (Continuous Capitalization): Because the premium C is paid upfront at time t = 0 and the option expires at maturity T, the exact economic breakeven taking into account the opportunity cost of cash at risk-free rate r is:
SBE (Call) = K + C · erT   |   SBE (Put) = K − P · erT
2. Mid-Life Breakeven (t < T): Prior to expiration, options hold positive Time Value. Therefore, if you hold a long call, you can sell it back to the market at a net profit/breakeven at an underlying spot price significantly lower than K + C!
Q-OD-04
Answered
If you make money when the underlying goes down both when you are long put and short call, what is the difference between the two positions?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Core Distinction: Payoff Profile & Downside Capture

While both positions are bearish (positive P&L when the underlying drops), their payoff structures, risk profiles, and Greeks differ fundamentally:

  • Long Put (Directional Downside Capture): When the stock drops further below K, your profit grows linearly up to a maximum of K − P (as ST → 0). You capture 100% of the ongoing downward move.
  • Short Call (Income / Yield Strategy): When the stock drops below K, the option expires worthless and your profit is strictly capped / constant at the premium received (+C). No matter how deep the market crashes, you can never earn more than $+C$.

⚖️ Comprehensive Comparison Table for Interviewers

Feature / Metric Long Put (Buy Put) Short Call (Sell Call)
Max Profit (if S drops to 0) Substantial (K − P) Capped at Premium (+C)
Max Loss (if S rallies to +∞) Capped at Premium Paid (−P) Infinite (−∞) ⚠️
Delta (Directional Bias) Negative (−Δ approaching −1) Negative (−Δ approaching 0 OTM)
Gamma & Volatility (Vega) Long Gamma (+Γ), Long Vega (+V) Short Gamma (−Γ), Short Vega (−V)
Upfront Cashflow Cash Outflow (Debit / pay P) Cash Inflow (Credit / receive C)
💡 Interview Pro-Tip (When to trade which):
• Buy a Long Put when you anticipate a violent market crash or high-volatility sell-off (you want long Gamma and long Vega to amplify gains).
• Sell a Short Call when you have a neutral-to-moderately bearish view in a stagnant market where implied volatility is overpriced (you want positive Theta / time decay and short Vega to harvest premium).
Q-OD-05
Answered
When and why would you exercise an American option before its expiry? Is the reasoning different for calls vs puts?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Golden Rule: Why Early Exercise is Normally Optimal to Avoid

An option's market price is composed of Intrinsic Value + Time Value (TV > 0). If you early exercise, you only capture the Intrinsic Value and destroy 100% of the remaining Time Value. Therefore, if you wish to exit or monetize an option position prior to maturity, it is almost always mathematically superior to sell the option in the market rather than exercising it.

However, early exercise becomes optimal under specific boundary conditions, and the reasoning differs completely between Calls and Puts:

1. American CALL Options (Trigger: Dividends)

  • Non-Dividend Paying Stocks: You should NEVER early exercise an American call on a non-dividend stock (Merton's Theorem). Why pay cash (K) early when you can keep your cash earning risk-free interest in the bank until maturity?
  • Dividend-Paying Stocks (D): When a stock goes ex-dividend, its share price drops by D. Call option holders do not receive cash dividends, so the call loses value when the stock drops across the ex-dividend date!
  • Mathematical Trigger: You early exercise a call just before the ex-dividend date (last cum-dividend day) if the upcoming dividend D exceeds the remaining time value plus the interest lost on paying strike K early:
    Exercise Call if: D > Time Value + K · (1 − e−r·Δt)

2. American PUT Options (Trigger: Deep ITM + Interest Rates)

  • Does it require dividends? NO! It can be optimal to early exercise an American put even on non-dividend paying stocks!
  • The Time Value of Money Logic: Suppose you own a Put with Strike K = $100, and the company goes bankrupt (S ≈ $1). Your intrinsic value is $99. Since stock prices cannot drop below $0, your intrinsic value cannot grow any larger than $100! If you wait until maturity T to receive that cash, your funds sit dead earning zero interest. If you exercise early today, you receive $99 cash immediately and deposit it to earn risk-free interest r until maturity!
  • Mathematical Trigger: Exercise when the put is Deep In-The-Money (ITM) and the interest earned on cash received exceeds the remaining time value of the put.
🎯 Quick Summary for Whiteboard:
• American Call: Early exercise is driven purely by Dividends (avoiding the ex-dividend stock drop).
• American Put: Early exercise is driven purely by Time Value of Money / Interest Rates (monetizing deep ITM cash immediately to earn interest).
Q-OD-06
Answered
What is a call with strike K = 0? What is the difference between holding such a call and holding the underlying stock directly?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Elevator Pitch & Real-World Trading Names

A Call option with Strike K = 0 has a payoff at maturity equal to max(ST − 0, 0) = ST. Because stock prices cannot be negative, the gross payoff is exactly equal to the spot price of the stock at maturity.

Real-World Trading Names (How to impress your interviewer):
• Prepaid Forward Contract / LEPO (Low Exercise Price Option): Traded extensively on European and Australian exchanges where investors pay the entire forward price upfront.
• Delta-One Access Products / Participatory Notes (P-Notes): Used heavily by global investment banks (Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley) to give hedge funds 100% economic exposure (Δ = 1) to restricted foreign equity markets (e.g., India, China A-shares, Taiwan) where local capital controls or stamp duties prevent direct share ownership.

⚖️ 4 Core Differences vs. Holding Underlying Stock Directly

Dimension Holding Underlying Stock Directly Holding Zero-Strike Call (K = 0)
1. Dividends (q) Receives all cash dividends quarterly right into bank account. Does NOT receive cash dividends paid during the option life. Thus, its upfront price is discounted by expected dividends: C0 = S0 · e−qT.
2. Voting Rights Full shareholder voting rights & corporate governance voice. No voting rights whatsoever.
3. Upfront Price (if No Divs) Exactly equal to spot price S0. Exactly equal to spot price S0 (by Put-Call Parity: C − P = S − K·e−rT with K=0, P=0).
4. Counterparty Credit Risk Direct physical/custodial asset ownership. Faces counterparty credit risk of the issuing bank or clearinghouse! ⚠️
💡 Interview Pro-Tip: If asked, "If a stock pays no dividends, why would anyone buy K=0 call instead of stock?"
Answer: To save on local transaction taxes/stamp duties, to bypass foreign ownership quotas (using Delta-One P-Notes), or to obtain leverage/margin efficiencies within prime brokerage accounts!
Q-OD-07
Answered
Breakdown the value of an option into its components. What is intrinsic value vs time value?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Fundamental Value Equation

The total market price (premium) of any option is always decomposed into two distinct components:

Total Option Value = Intrinsic Value (IV) + Time Value (Extrinsic Value, TV)

1. Intrinsic Value (IV) — Immediate Exercise Payoff

The value you would capture if you exercised the option right now at the current spot price S. Intrinsic value is strictly non-negative (IV ≥ 0; it can never be less than zero because you are never forced to exercise).

  • Call Intrinsic Value: IV = max(S − K, 0)
  • Put Intrinsic Value: IV = max(K − S, 0)

2. Time Value (TV / Extrinsic Value) — Optionality & Uncertainty

The excess premium above the intrinsic value that the market is willing to pay for the probability / optionality that the underlying spot price will move further in a favorable direction prior to expiration T.

  • Key Drivers: Time Value depends directly on Implied Volatility (σ) (more dispersion = higher chance of large payoff) and Time Remaining to Expiration (τ) (more time = more opportunity to move).
  • Peak Time Value: Time value is strictly maximized when the option is exactly At-The-Money (S = K) because uncertainty about ending ITM vs OTM is at its highest (Delta ≈ 0.50, Gamma & Vega peak).
🎯 Advanced Quant Trap (Can Time Value be NEGATIVE?):
While time value is strictly positive (TV > 0) for American options (since early exercise guarantees at least intrinsic value) and European Calls without dividends, Time Value can actually be NEGATIVE for Deep In-The-Money European Put Options!
Why? Because if a European Put is deep ITM (e.g., S ≈ $1, K = $100), you cannot exercise until maturity T. Due to discounting / time value of money, the present value of the guaranteed $100 strike you receive in the future (K · e−rT) is less than $100 today! Thus, the market price falls below intrinsic value (P < K − S), giving a negative time value!

Q-OD-08
Answered
What are all the parameters that influence the price of an option? For each one, explain how an increase affects the price of a call and a put.
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The 6 Core Pricing Parameters (Black-Scholes / Binomial Inputs)

Every option pricing model relies on exactly 6 underlying parameters. When one parameter increases (holding all others constant), here is how Call and Put prices respond:

Parameter (When Increased ↑) Call Price Effect Put Price Effect Economic Intuition & Mechanism
1. Underlying Spot Price (S ↑) Increase ↑ (+Δ) Decrease ↓ (−Δ) Calls gain intrinsic/time value as probability of ending ITM rises. Puts lose value as they move OTM.
2. Strike Price (K ↑) Decrease ↓ Increase ↑ Higher strike means paying more to buy stock (bad for Calls) or receiving more when selling stock (good for Puts).
3. Implied Volatility (σ ↑) Increase ↑ (+V) Increase ↑ (+V) Asymmetric Payoff Convexity: Higher dispersion widens both tails. You capture large upside gains while losses remain bounded at $0 on the downside. Thus, both Calls and Puts gain value!
4. Time to Expiration (T ↑) Increase ↑ (+Θ)* Increase ↑ (+Θ)* More time allows greater probability for the stock to make a large favorable move. (*Nuance: Deep ITM European Puts or high-dividend options can lose value with more time due to discounting/carry).
5. Risk-Free Interest Rate (r ↑) Increase ↑ (+ρ) Decrease ↓ (−ρ) Time Value of Money: Call buyers defer paying K until maturity (cash earns interest in bank; PV of strike K·e−rT drops). Put buyers wait to receive cash K (heavy discounting reduces its PV today).
6. Dividend Yield (q ↑) Decrease ↓ Increase ↑ Dividends cause the underlying stock price to drop across ex-dividend dates (S·e−qT). Lower future stock prices hurt Calls and boost Puts.
💡 Whiteboard Etiquette: Never just memorize the arrows (+ − + + + −)! When an interviewer asks you about parameters, always start by stating: "The 6 parameters are S, K, σ, T, r, q..." and immediately follow up with the economic intuition for Why Volatility and Rates work the way they do, as those are the two most frequently probed follow-ups.
Q-OD-09
Answered
If interest rates increase, what happens to the price of a call? Why? (Explain economically, not just from the formula.)
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Spot-Forward Parity / Carry Explanation (10/10 Quant Answer)

When interest rates increase, the price of a Call option increases (↑, positive Rho ρ > 0) while the price of a Put option decreases (↓, negative Rho ρ < 0).

Instead of just reciting the Black-Scholes formula or discounting the strike price, the cleanest economic framework to explain why to an interviewer is via Spot-Forward Parity:

Forward Price: FT = S0 · e(r − q)T
  • The Forward Channel: When interest rate r increases, the expected forward price of the stock (FT) strictly rises!
  • Economic / Cashflow Intuition: Buying a call gives you the right to acquire the underlying stock at maturity T without paying for it today at time t = 0. Instead of buying the stock outright (which ties up cash S0 or requires borrowing at rate r), you keep your cash deposited in the bank earning interest rate r! The higher the interest rate, the greater the carry / interest earned on your cash reserve during the option's life. Because you defer paying the fixed strike hurdle K while your alternative cash balance grows faster, the Call becomes more valuable (↑).
  • Conversely for Puts: Holding a put means you are waiting until maturity T to receive cash K in exchange for stock. Higher interest rates heavily penalize future cash inflows due to heavier discounting (PV(K) = K e−rT ↓), and you forgo the interest you could have earned if you sold the stock for cash right now. Thus, Puts lose value (↓).
💡 Interview Pro-Tip: Always mention that Rho (ρ) is generally the least influential Greek for short-dated options (e.g., 1-month options), but becomes extremely powerful for long-dated options / LEAPS (e.g., 2 to 5 years maturity) where compounding carry over time T dominates the valuation!
Q-OD-10
Answered
How do dividends affect the price of a call option? And a put option? Why?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Immediate Price Impact & Forward Channel

If a stock announces an unexpectedly high dividend (or higher continuous dividend yield q ↑), the price of its Call options decreases (↓) immediately, while the price of its Put options increases (↑).

The Economic & Mathematical Mechanism:

  • Forward Price Drop across Ex-Dividend Dates: On the ex-dividend date, a stock price mechanically drops by the dividend amount D because the cash leaves the company's balance sheet. Mathematically, the forward price is:
    FT = (S0 − PV(Dividends)) · erT   or   FT = S0 · e(r − q)T
  • Call Impact (↓): Call holders do not receive cash dividends. When a surprise dividend is announced, the present value of future stock prices (FT) falls relative to the fixed strike K. The probability of ending deep in-the-money drops, causing Call prices to fall immediately.
  • Put Impact (↑): Put holders benefit when the stock falls. A higher dividend means the spot price will drop across the ex-dividend date, increasing the probability and magnitude of the Put ending in-the-money. Thus, Put prices jump immediately.
🎯 Trading Desk & Market Making Gotcha (How to score bonus points):
1. Early Exercise Risk: A surprise dividend spike dramatically increases the optimal probability of early exercising American Call options right on the cum-dividend date to capture the dividend before the stock drops!
2. Are options dividend-protected? Standard ordinary cash dividends are not adjusted on exchange-traded options (so option holders absorb the full price drop/gain). Only extraordinary or special dividends (typically > 10% of stock value) trigger exchange adjustments (adjusting strike K and multiplier) by clearinghouses like OCC or Eurex to protect option holders.
Q-OD-11
Answered
Why is an option with high volatility more expensive than one with low volatility, all else being equal?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Bounded Downside / Payoff Asymmetry Explanation

A classic beginner mistake is saying "higher volatility means the option is more likely to end in-the-money." This is absolutely false. Under lognormal distribution, higher volatility pushes the median and mode of the distribution down, which can actually decrease the pure probability of an ATM/OTM call ending ITM.

The true reason volatility increases option prices is Payoff Asymmetry (Convexity):

  • Bounded Downside: As an option buyer, your maximum loss is strictly capped at the premium paid ($0 intrinsic value), no matter how far out-of-the-money the underlying goes.
  • Unbounded Upside: Your potential gain on the upside is theoretically unlimited (for calls) or very large (for puts).
  • The Volatility Effect: Higher volatility widens both tails of the probability distribution (fatter tails). The underlying asset is more likely to experience extreme moves in both directions. Because you are fully protected from extreme moves in the "wrong" direction (loss capped at 0) but fully participate in extreme moves in the "right" direction, the Expected Value (probability × magnitude) of the payoff is strictly higher!
💡 Interview Pro-Tip: In one sentence: "We don't pay for a higher probability of winning; we pay for the fact that when we do win, the magnitude of the win is astronomically larger, while our losses remain strictly floored at zero."
Q-OD-12
Answered
You have two ATM calls with the same strike and spot but different maturities: 2 years and 6 months. Do they both have a delta of 0.5? Which one has the higher delta?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Lognormal Skew Brainteaser

This is a famous Quant brainteaser that tests your understanding of the Black-Scholes d1 term and lognormal distributions.

  1. Is ATM Delta exactly 0.50? No.
    In Black-Scholes, Call Delta is N(d1). Stock prices follow a lognormal distribution (bounded by 0, unbounded to the upside), meaning the distribution is right-skewed. Because of this right-skew (the geometric drift component +½σ2T), the center of mass shifts. For an ATM call (S = K) with zero rates/dividends, d1 = ½σ√T. Since d1 is strictly positive, N(d1) is strictly greater than 0.50.
  2. Which maturity has the higher Delta? The 2-Year Call.
    Since d1 = ½σ√T (assuming zero rates/dividends for simplicity), as maturity T increases, d1 increases. As d1 increases, N(d1) gets larger. Therefore, the 2-year ATM call has a higher Delta than the 6-month ATM call.
💡 Intuition: Give a stock more time (2 years vs 6 months), and the lognormal positive skewness becomes much more pronounced (more time to compound up to infinity, but still bounded at 0). The expected forward price of the stock is pulled higher by that massive right tail, thus increasing the expected payoff and the Delta of the Call.
Q-OD-13
Answered
Compare a call ATM and a put ATM with 1-year maturity on a stock that pays no dividend. Which costs more if rates are zero? And if rates are 10%?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Put-Call Parity Application

This is a classic question that requires you to use the Put-Call Parity equation to compare prices directly. The equation is:
C − P = S − K · e−rT

  • Scenario 1: Rates are 0% (r = 0)
    If r = 0, then e−0 = 1. The equation becomes C − P = S − K.
    Since the options are At-The-Money (ATM), S = K. Therefore, C − P = 0, which means Call Price = Put Price. Without the time value of money, there is no carry advantage to deferring the stock purchase.
  • Scenario 2: Rates are 10% (r > 0)
    If r = 10%, the present value of the strike price is heavily discounted (K · e−0.10 < K).
    The equation becomes C − P = S − (something less than S), which means C − P > 0.
    Therefore, Call Price > Put Price.
💡 Interview Pro-Tip: The fastest way to answer this intuitively without algebra is via the Forward Price. When r > 0, the forward price F = S · erT is strictly greater than the spot price. Since the Forward price is higher than the ATM strike K, the Call is effectively "in-the-money" relative to the forward, making it more expensive than the Put.

⚖️ Put-Call Parity

Q-OD-14
Answered
State and explain the put-call parity. Write the formula with and without dividends.
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Core Equation of Option Pricing

Put-Call Parity defines the strict mathematical relationship between the price of a European Call and a European Put with the exact same strike K and maturity T. It prevents arbitrage between the options market and the spot market.

Without Dividends: C + K · e−rT = P + S
With Dividends: C + K · e−rT = P + S · e−qT

Economic Explanation (The Replicating Portfolios)

The equation states that two specific portfolios have the exact same payoff at maturity T, and therefore must cost the same today:

  • Left Side (Fiduciary Call): C + K · e−rT
    You buy a Call option and deposit enough cash in the bank to perfectly cover the strike price at maturity. At expiration, if the stock goes up, you exercise the call and own the stock. If it goes down, the call expires worthless but you have cash K. Payoff = max(ST, K).
  • Right Side (Protective Put): P + S
    You buy the underlying stock and buy a Put option to protect against downside. At expiration, if the stock goes up, you keep the stock. If the stock crashes, the put guarantees you can sell it for K. Payoff = max(ST, K).
⚠️ Critical Condition: Put-Call parity strictly holds only for European options! For American options, because of the possibility of early exercise, the parity becomes an inequality boundary: S − K ≤ C − P ≤ S − K · e−rT.
Q-OD-15
Answered
How can you replicate a forward using options? What is a synthetic forward?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Synthetic Forward Structure

To perfectly replicate a long forward contract (a commitment to buy the asset at price K at time T), you build a Synthetic Long Forward using European options by:

  • Buying (Long) a Call at strike K and maturity T.
  • Selling (Short) a Put at the same strike K and maturity T.

Why the Payoff is Identical:

At expiration T, there are only two scenarios:

  1. If ST > K: The short put expires worthless. You exercise your long call and buy the asset for K.
  2. If ST < K: Your long call expires worthless. The put buyer exercises their option against you, forcing you to buy the asset for K.

In both scenarios, you are forced to buy the asset exactly at price K, which is the exact mathematical definition and payoff of a Long Forward contract (ST − K).

💡 Interview Pro-Tip: You can quickly prove this using Put-Call parity. Rearrange C − P = S − K · e−rT. The left side (C − P) is your Synthetic Forward position. The right side is the present value of a forward contract. If you set K equal to the forward price FT, the cost of entering this synthetic position is exactly zero!
Q-OD-16
Answered
I buy a call and sell a put at the same strike. What is my position? What are the delta and vega of this combined position?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Position: Synthetic Long Forward

Buying a call (+C) and selling a put (−P) at the exact same strike and maturity creates a Synthetic Long Forward. (See Q-OD-15).

📌 The Delta and Vega Trap

Interviewers ask this specific follow-up to test if you mechanically memorize Greeks or if you truly understand what the position represents.

  • Delta is 1.0 (or 100):
    A common trap is assuming the Delta is 0 because the options "cancel out." False!
    A long call has a positive Delta (+ΔC). A long put has a negative Delta (−ΔP). Since you are shorting the put, your Delta becomes −(−ΔP) = +ΔP.
    By Put-Call parity, the derivative with respect to Spot is ΔCall − ΔPut = 1. You have created a synthetic stock/forward position, so it behaves exactly like holding 1 share of the underlying asset. Therefore, Delta = 1.0.
  • Vega is 0:
    A long call has positive Vega. A long put has positive Vega. Because European Call and Put Vegas are mathematically identical at the same strike and maturity (VegaC = VegaP), buying the call (+Vega) and selling the put (−Vega) perfectly cancel each other out.
    Therefore, Vega = 0. The position has zero exposure to implied volatility.
💡 Intuition: Since a Forward contract is a linear, delta-one product with zero optionality, it cannot have Vega. Since you successfully replicated a forward, your synthetic position must also be a linear delta-one product with zero Vega!
Q-OD-17
Answered
Would you prefer a forward or a call with strike 0? Why?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Funding Cost & Counterparty Risk Argument

You would strongly prefer the Forward contract. Here is why:

  • The Payoffs are Identical: A call option with strike K = 0 has a payoff of max(ST − 0, 0) = ST. A forward contract (if struck at a price of 0, essentially just buying the asset outright at maturity) gives you the asset. Both derivatives result in you owning the asset at maturity.
  • The Premium (Cash Outflow Timing):
    • Zero-Strike Call: You must pay the premium upfront today (t=0). Since the payoff is strictly ST, the premium is the present value of the stock, S0. You are tying up your capital and losing out on the risk-free rate.
    • Forward Contract: Forward contracts have an initial value of strictly zero. You pay absolutely nothing today. You only exchange cash at maturity T.
  • Counterparty Risk: If you buy a zero-strike call, you pay the full stock price today to the option writer. If the writer defaults or goes bankrupt before maturity, you lose your entire investment. With a forward, no principal is exchanged upfront, minimizing counterparty credit risk.
💡 Interview Pro-Tip: Time value of money (funding cost) and counterparty credit risk are the two most fundamental concepts in derivatives trading. Whenever asked to choose between two identical payoffs where one requires upfront cash and the other requires deferred cash, always choose the deferred payment to capture the "carry" (interest).

📊 Black-Scholes Model

Q-OD-18
Answered
What are the assumptions of the Black-Scholes model? List all of them.
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The 7 Pillars of Black-Scholes (1973)

To derive the analytical solution for option pricing, Black and Scholes made several strict theoretical assumptions. You should be able to rattle these off quickly in an interview:

  1. Lognormal Distribution of Prices: Stock returns are normally distributed, meaning stock prices follow a Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM) with constant drift (μ) and constant volatility (σ). Prices cannot be negative.
  2. Continuous Trading: Markets are open 24/7. Trading happens continuously in time, meaning prices move continuously without jumps or gaps.
  3. No Market Frictions: There are no transaction costs, no taxes, no bid-ask spreads, and assets are perfectly divisible (fractional shares exist).
  4. Constant Risk-Free Rate: The risk-free interest rate (r) is constant and known over the life of the option. Participants can borrow and lend unlimited amounts of cash at this exact same rate.
  5. Unrestricted Short Selling: Investors can short sell the underlying asset without penalty and have full use of the short sale proceeds.
  6. No Arbitrage: The market is perfectly efficient and free of riskless arbitrage opportunities.
  7. No Dividends (Original Model): The underlying asset pays no dividends during the life of the option. (Note: Robert Merton extended the model in 1973 to account for a continuous dividend yield, q).
💡 Interview Pro-Tip: The most heavily scrutinized assumption is #1 (Constant Volatility and Lognormal Returns). Interviewers will always ask a follow-up about why this is false in reality (volatility smiles/smirks, fat tails, jump risk). Be prepared to pivot directly into the realities of the volatility surface!
Q-OD-19
Answered
Which of the Black-Scholes assumptions are false in reality? What are the practical implications?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Reality Check: Why Black-Scholes Fails

Strictly speaking, every single assumption of the Black-Scholes model is false in reality. However, the most egregious and practically important failure is the assumption of constant volatility and lognormal returns.

  • False Assumption: Constant Volatility.
    In reality, volatility is not constant. It is stochastic (it changes over time) and exhibits "clustering" (high volatility periods are followed by high volatility, low by low).
  • False Assumption: Lognormal Returns (Normal Distribution).
    In reality, asset returns exhibit fat tails (excess kurtosis) and negative skewness. Market crashes happen far more frequently than a normal distribution predicts.

Practical Implications (The Volatility Smile)

Because Black-Scholes assumes a normal distribution, it severely underestimates the probability of extreme tail events (market crashes). Therefore, if you price deep out-of-the-money (OTM) put options using the Black-Scholes formula, the model price will be much lower than the actual market price.

To force the Black-Scholes model to output the true market price of these OTM puts, traders have to plug in a much higher implied volatility. This creates the famous Volatility Skew / Smile, proving that the market assigns a higher probability to extreme events than the model does.

💡 Interview Pro-Tip: Also mention that continuous trading is false (markets close over the weekend, leading to gap risk) and frictionless markets are false (bid-ask spreads and transaction costs make continuous delta-hedging ruinously expensive).
Q-OD-20
Answered
Give the closed-form Black-Scholes formula for pricing a call. What is the difference between N(d1) and N(d2)?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Formula

For a non-dividend paying stock, the Black-Scholes formula for a European Call is:

C = S0 · N(d1) − K · e−rT · N(d2)

📌 What do N(d1) and N(d2) actually mean?

Both N(d1) and N(d2) are cumulative standard normal probabilities (ranging from 0 to 1). But they represent fundamentally different economic concepts:

  • N(d2): The Risk-Neutral Probability of Exercising
    N(d2) is simply the probability that the option finishes in-the-money (ST > K) in a risk-neutral world. Therefore, the second term K · e−rT · N(d2) represents the expected present value of the cash you will pay at expiration to buy the stock.
  • N(d1): The Delta (and Conditional Expected Value)
    N(d1) is the Delta of the call option (how much the option price moves for a $1 move in the stock). Economically, it represents the conditional expected value of the asset given that the option expires in-the-money. The first term S0 · N(d1) is the expected present value of the stock you will receive.
💡 Interview Pro-Tip: The entire formula can be read in plain English as: Call Price = [Expected PV of Stock Received] − [Expected PV of Cash Paid]. Understanding this structure is far more impressive to an interviewer than just blindly reciting the formula!
Q-OD-21
Answered
Which is the only unknown variable in the Black-Scholes formula? How do you obtain it?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Implied Volatility

The only unobservable unknown variable in the Black-Scholes model is Volatility (σ).

  • Observable Variables: Spot price (S), Strike (K), Time to maturity (T), and Risk-free rate (r) are all known, observable market parameters.
  • The Option Price (C) is also observable! Because options trade actively on exchanges, we know the true market price of the option.

How to obtain it:

Because we know the market price of the option (Cmarket), we plug it into the left side of the Black-Scholes equation. We then back-solve for σ, which gives us the Implied Volatility (the volatility implied by the market price).

Since the Black-Scholes formula cannot be algebraically rearranged to isolate σ (there is no closed-form inverse), we must solve for it numerically using root-finding algorithms, most notably the Newton-Raphson method or the Bisection method.

Q-OD-22
Answered
Price a call from memory: Spot = 100, Vol = 20%, T = 1Y, ATM. What is the approximate price? Where does the simplified formula S₀ × σ × √T × 0.4 come from?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The "Trader's Rule of Thumb" Pricing

Using the Brenner-Subrahmanyam (1988) approximation formula for an ATM option:

Call ≈ Put ≈ 0.4 · S0 · σ · √T

Plugging in the numbers (S0 = 100, σ = 0.20, T = 1):
Price = 0.4 × 100 × 0.20 × 1 = 8.00

Where does the 0.4 come from? (The Mathematical Proof)

For an ATM option (S = K) where interest rates are zero (r = 0), the d1 formula simplifies heavily:

  • d1 = (½σ2T) / (σ√T) = ½σ√T
  • d2 = −½σ√T (Notice that d2 = −d1)

The Black-Scholes formula simplifies to: C = S[N(d1) − N(−d1)]. Because of normal distribution symmetry, N(−d1) = 1 − N(d1).
Substitute this in: C = S[2N(d1) − 1].

The Magic Taylor Expansion:
We approximate the standard normal CDF N(x) around zero using a first-order Taylor expansion: N(x) ≈ 0.5 + x/√(2π).
So, N(d1) ≈ 0.5 + d1/√(2π).
Plug this into our simplified Call formula:
C ≈ S · [2(0.5 + d1/√(2π)) − 1] = S · [2d1/√(2π)].

Finally, substitute d1 = ½σ√T back into the equation:
C ≈ S · [2(½σ√T) / √(2π)] = S · σ√T / √(2π)

Since 1 / √(2π) = 1 / √6.28 ≈ 0.3989 (which is basically 0.4), the final approximation is:
C ≈ 0.4 · S · σ · √T

Q-OD-23
Answered
What are other models besides Black-Scholes for pricing options? Compare the pros and cons of B&S, Monte Carlo, and binomial trees for pricing a simple cap.
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Alternative Pricing Models (Pros & Cons)

  • 1. Black-Scholes Model (Analytical)
    ✅ Pros: Closed-form analytical formula. Instantaneous computation. Very easy to compute Greeks.
    ❌ Cons: Relies on highly restrictive, unrealistic assumptions (constant volatility, lognormal returns). Cannot price American options (early exercise). Cannot easily handle complex path-dependent features.
  • 2. Binomial / Trinomial Trees (Lattice Models)
    ✅ Pros: Discretizes time, making it very easy to visualize. The biggest advantage is that it can price American options because it checks for optimal early exercise at every single node working backward from expiration.
    ❌ Cons: Computationally slow for large time steps. Becomes exponentially complex (curse of dimensionality) if you try to price options on multiple underlying assets (basket options).
  • 3. Monte Carlo Simulation (Probabilistic)
    ✅ Pros: Incredibly flexible. You can use any custom probability distribution (fat tails, jump-diffusion, stochastic volatility). It is the only reliable way to price highly complex path-dependent options (e.g., Asian options, Barrier options) and multi-asset derivatives.
    ❌ Cons: Extremely computationally intensive. Converges very slowly (error shrinks at a rate of 1/√N). Standard Monte Carlo struggles to price American options because it simulates paths forward, whereas early exercise requires backward induction (though the Longstaff-Schwartz algorithm solves this at a high computational cost).
Q-OD-24
Answered
Price this call using a one-period binomial tree: K=100, S=90, σ=20%, r=0, T=1, probability of up = probability of down = 1/2.
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Step-by-Step Mental Math Pricing

If you don't have a calculator, you can solve this using the expected value in a risk-neutral world.

  1. Find the Risk-Neutral Expected Value of the Stock:
    Since the risk-free rate r = 0, the expected value of the stock in a risk-neutral world must simply be the current spot price: E[ST] = 90.
  2. Calculate the Up and Down Node Prices:
    To match the volatility σ = 20%, we can use the simple discrete approximation: Sup = S(1 + σ) and Sdown = S(1 − σ).
    Up Price: 90 × 1.20 = 108
    Down Price: 90 × 0.80 = 72
    Check: 50% probability of 108 + 50% probability of 72 = 54 + 36 = 90. Perfect! The tree is risk-neutral.
  3. Calculate the Option Payoffs at the Nodes:
    The Strike (K) is 100.
    Up Node Payoff: max(108 − 100, 0) = 8
    Down Node Payoff: max(72 − 100, 0) = 0
  4. Calculate the Expected Option Value:
    Expected Payoff = (50% × 8) + (50% × 0) = 4.
    Since r = 0, the discount factor is 1.
    Final Call Price = 4

🔄 Forwards & Futures

Q-OD-25
Answered
What is a forward? What is a future? What are the key differences between the two?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Core Definitions

Both are derivative contracts that obligate two parties to buy/sell an underlying asset at a pre-agreed price on a specific future date.

  • Forward: A highly customizable, private Over-The-Counter (OTC) contract between two institutional counterparties.
  • Future: A highly standardized contract traded on a centralized exchange (like the CME or Eurex).

📌 The 3 Key Differences to Memorize

Feature Forward (OTC) Future (Exchange)
Customization 100% bespoke (custom dates, sizes, assets). Rigidly standardized (fixed maturities, tick sizes, lot sizes).
Credit / Default Risk High counterparty risk (if the other bank goes bust, you lose). Zero counterparty risk. The exchange clearinghouse acts as the counterparty to both sides.
Settlement Settled entirely at maturity (one big cash flow at the end). Marked-to-market and settled daily (via margin accounts).
Q-OD-26
Answered
How do you price a forward? What are the components (spot, rates, dividends, repo)?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Cost of Carry Model

The price of a forward contract is entirely determined by arbitrage against the spot market. It has absolutely nothing to do with where traders "predict" the price will go. The formula is:

Forward = Spot × e(Cost of Carry) × T

The Components of Carry:

If you want to synthesize a forward, you borrow cash, buy the physical spot asset today, and hold it until maturity. The Forward price must perfectly offset the costs and benefits of doing this:

  • Spot Price (S): The baseline price of the asset today.
  • Interest Rates (r): (Increases Forward Price). If you buy spot, you must borrow cash and pay interest. If you buy a forward, you keep your cash in the bank and earn interest. The forward must be priced higher to reflect this funding cost.
  • Dividends / Yield (q): (Decreases Forward Price). If you hold the spot stock, you receive cash dividends. If you hold the forward, you do not. The forward must be priced lower to compensate for the missed income.
  • Repo Rate / Borrow Cost: (Decreases Forward Price). To short a stock, you must borrow it from a prime broker and pay a "repo" or borrow fee. High borrow costs (e.g., hard-to-borrow meme stocks) push the forward price down significantly, sometimes causing it to trade at a deep discount to spot.

Final Equity Formula: F = S0 · e(r − q − borrow_cost)T

Q-OD-27
Answered
What is the difference between an option and a forward/future?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 Contingent Claim vs. Forward Commitment

The difference perfectly aligns with the CFA curriculum's distinction between the two major classes of derivatives:

  • Options are Contingent Claims: You have the right, but not the obligation, to exercise the contract. Because you are buying optionality (the ability to walk away if the market moves against you), you must pay an upfront Premium to the seller.
  • Forwards/Futures are Forward Commitments: They are legally binding obligations to transact at the maturity date, regardless of where the market price is. Because there is no optionality, they are entered into at zero cost. There is No Premium.
💡 Interview Pro-Tip: The exact terminology you want to use here is "Asymmetric Payoff" (Options) versus "Symmetric Payoff" (Forwards/Futures). Options truncate your downside risk at the cost of an initial premium.
Q-OD-28
Answered
Which is more expensive, a future or a forward? Why?
💡 View Answer & Interview Tips

📌 The Daily Settlement & Interest Rate Correlation Trap

This is a classic Quant trap. If interest rates are perfectly constant and deterministic, the price of a Future and a Forward are mathematically identical. However, because interest rates are stochastic (random) in the real world, the prices diverge based on Settlement Timing.

Recall from Q-OD-25 that Futures settle daily (Marked-to-Market), while Forwards settle at maturity. Therefore, the pricing difference comes down entirely to the correlation between the underlying asset and interest rates.

  • Positive Correlation (Future > Forward): If the asset price and interest rates move in the same direction, the Future is more expensive. Why? If the asset goes up, your long Future makes money and deposits cash into your margin account today. Because rates also went up, you can reinvest this cash at a higher interest rate! The daily settlement creates a massive reinvestment advantage.
  • Negative Correlation (Forward > Future): If the asset and rates move in opposite directions, the Forward is more expensive. Why? If the asset goes up and your Future makes money, rates go down, so you are forced to reinvest your daily winnings at a lower rate. You would rather wait until maturity (Forward) to lock in the payoff.
💡 Interview Pro-Tip: Memorize the mechanism: "Because Futures are marked-to-market daily, a positive correlation with interest rates creates a compounding reinvestment advantage for the Future, driving its price higher than the Forward."
